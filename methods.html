Tunisia Earth Watch: methods and limits

Last updated 11 October 2026 · Back to the map

Read this first. This is a demonstration tool. It is not an official warning service and has only been tested in part against past events (section 5). Do not use it to decide whether to evacuate, travel, plant, irrigate or spend money. For official warnings follow the Institut National de la Météorologie (INM) and the national civil protection authority. For farming advice ask your regional agriculture office (CRDA).

1. What each part of the app is

  • Satellite layers (vegetation, water, trend, urban, flood susceptibility, fire history) describe the past and present land surface. They are maps, not predictions.
  • Climate-vegetation zones (machine learning) group the country by how vegetation and water change through the year. They are a map of 2025, not a prediction.
  • Projections (years 2026 to 2032 and the rainfall and irrigation sliders) are simple what-if calculations, not forecasts.
  • Live forecasts (16-day rain, river flow, the heavy-rain watch and the fire-danger watch) come from weather models and change every day.
  • Watches point to days and regions that deserve attention. They never say that a flood or fire will happen.

3. How the numbers are built

  • NDVI and NDWI are read from the red channel of small PNG images exported from Earth Engine (NDVI from −0.2 to 0.8, NDWI from −0.6 to 0.4).
  • Water stress score (demo index) = 100 × (1 − 0.8 × NDVI term − 0.2 × NDWI term), clamped to 0 to 100. Low is below 25, moderate 25 to 49, high 50 to 74, severe 75 and above. It is a relative index based on vegetation only. It is not drought, soil moisture or water supply, and it rates bare desert as stressed. It does not describe cities.
  • Projection for a later year = 2025 value + trend × years. The rainfall slider multiplies NDVI by (1 + 0.5 × rainfall change) and the irrigation slider adds a small boost where vegetation is sparse. These coefficients are assumptions, not measured. The shaded band on the outlook chart widens with the square root of the years and is illustrative, not a statistical confidence interval.
  • Surface class (water, urban, dense, cropland, steppe, bare) uses fixed NDVI, NDWI and built-up thresholds. The spectral curve shown is a typical reference for that class, not a measurement.
  • Flood susceptibility = 50% low-lying (height above the local low point within 2 km), 30% flat ground, 20% near places where water has been seen. Permanent water is set to zero. The weights are assumptions. It shows where water would collect if heavy rain fell. It does not say how often it rains, so large flat deserts score high.
  • Fire history counts days with at least one satellite fire detection per cell. It includes gas flares and crop burning, and it can miss small fires.
  • Heavy-rain watch (per governorate centre): Attention if rain is 15 mm or more in a day or 30 mm or more over two days. Watch if 30 mm or more in a day or 50 mm or more over two days, or Attention in a high flood-susceptibility area. These values are demonstration values.
  • Fire-danger watch counts three factors on a day: maximum temperature 35°C or more, afternoon humidity 25% or less, wind 30 km/h or more. Three factors is Watch, two is Attention. A day with 5 mm or more of rain resets to none. The level goes up one step if fires were detected nearby and down one step if the area has little vegetation (yearly mean NDVI below 0.15). It is a simplified score, not the international Fire Weather Index. Its weather part was back-tested in section 5, with weak results. This is the rule the live app uses (version 1). The version 2 model in section 5 is not used in the app.
  • Confidence labels on watches depend only on how far ahead the day is: higher up to 3 days, medium up to 7, low beyond. This is a rule of thumb, not a measured accuracy.
  • Suggested actions in the forecast panel are generic draft text. They are not from Tunisian official guidance yet and the app says so.

4. Machine learning: climate-vegetation zones

The zones layer groups the country by how vegetation and water change through the year. It uses k-means clustering, an unsupervised method, so no labelled training data is involved.

Method

Each pixel on the 1 km grid gets a fingerprint of 24 numbers: its NDVI for each of the 12 months of 2025, followed by its NDWI for each month (the same Sentinel-2 monthly medians used elsewhere in the app). K-means (k-means++ initialisation, fixed random seed so results are repeatable, 4 to 8 zones, 6 by default) groups pixels with similar curves. It is trained on a sample of every fourth pixel in each direction, then every valid pixel is assigned to its nearest zone. Pixels with fewer than 9 clear months are skipped, and missing months in the remaining pixels are filled with that pixel's own mean. The calculation runs in the browser (kmeans.js) on the same PNG grids as the rest of the app.

How the zones are named

The algorithm only finds groups. The names, such as "Dense vegetation or forest", "Steppe and rangeland" or "Desert and bare soil", are added afterwards by simple thresholds on each zone's average NDVI and NDWI. They are an interpretation, not a model output. When two zones fall into the same name, the second is shown with "(2)".

Check against ESA WorldCover

The zones were compared with ESA WorldCover (2021), a satellite land-cover map, on the 1 km grid with 6 zones (about 168,000 pixels). The WorldCover class codes were exported from Earth Engine as data/worldcover.png, and the comparison runs in the browser after you click "Find zones".

About two thirds of Tunisia is desert, so equal-sized groups split the desert artificially, which makes the Rand index gap look larger than the real benefit. On purity and mutual information the zones are only about two points better.
  • Zone names agree with WorldCover for 87% of pixels, but the list of WorldCover classes counted as agreeing with each name is fixed in the code and is generous. One zone, "Steppe and rangeland", is 47% cropland in WorldCover, so that name fits poorly. The names come from simple thresholds, not from the model.
  • WorldCover is itself a satellite-derived map, not field data. Neighbouring pixels are not independent, so the figures are descriptive.

Limits of the zones

  • This is not a validated land-cover map. No labels were used to build it, and the comparison with WorldCover above shows modest agreement, not accuracy on the ground.
  • It describes one year (2025) of monthly composites, not a long-term climate.
  • At 1 km, small features such as sebkhas, chotts or oases can be absorbed into larger zones.
  • Results change with the number of zones chosen.
  • Informal check: the zones follow the expected north-to-south pattern (humid north, steppe in the centre, desert in the south). This is a visual sense-check, not a validation.

Relation to hyperspectral data

The prototype uses multispectral Sentinel-2 data. The same per-pixel fingerprint approach can take the 200+ bands of hyperspectral sources such as EnMAP, PRISMA or EMIT. These sensors are not used yet.

5. Validation status

The scores have been tested only in part. Machine-learning zones: compared with ESA WorldCover (2021), the zones reach a purity of 75% and a normalised mutual information of 0.37, against 73% and 0.35 for an NDVI-only baseline, so they add only a modest amount beyond vegetation level. Fire-danger watch, version 1 (weather rule only, without wind): tested on 6,710 governorate-days in the northern governorates, June to September 2021 to 2025, against NASA FIRMS fire detections. Fire was detected on 9.4% of flagged days and 5.4% of other days (1.75 times as often). The rule caught 20% of the days with a detection and 91% of flagged days had none, and the result changed a lot from year to year. Treat it as weak. Fire model, version 2 (back-test only, not in the app): a logistic model trained on 2003 to 2020 and tested on 2021 to 2025 ranks days better than version 1 (AUC 0.80 against 0.54), but most of that comes from which governorate and which part of summer it is, and it has not been tested with real forecasts. Heavy-rain watch, flood susceptibility and the water-stress score: not tested against past events. Treat them as demonstrations.

Fire-danger rule (version 1): back-test

We tested the weather part of the rule (maximum temperature 35°C or more, humidity 25% or less, rain under 5 mm) on every day from 1 June to 30 September, 2021 to 2025, in the 11 governorates whose centre lies north of 36°N (6,710 governorate-days). A fire counts when NASA FIRMS records at least one detection (confidence 50 or higher) in the same governorate on the same day. Weather is ERA5-Land daily reanalysis. The northern governorates were chosen to avoid the gas flares of the south. The script is scripts/07_fire_backtest.js and the counts are in results/fire_backtest_2021_2025.csv.

The ratio is how often a fire was detected on flagged days divided by how often on other days (1 means no better than chance).

  • Flagged days had a detection about 1.75 times as often as other days. A simple 95% interval is 1.4 to 2.2, but it ignores the link between neighbouring days and governorates, so it is too narrow.
  • The rule is better than chance overall but weak. It caught 20% of the days with a detection, 91% of flagged days had none, and the ratio ranged from 3.00 (2021) to 0.76 (2024).
  • A governorate counts as having a fire if there is a detection anywhere inside it, so larger governorates have a higher chance by size alone.
  • Not tested: the wind factor (ERA5-Land has no daily maximum wind), the adjustments for nearby fire history and low vegetation, and forecast skill, because reanalysis weather was used instead of the Open-Meteo forecast. Humidity was estimated from the daily-mean dewpoint and the daily-maximum temperature, which is an approximation. The app's full rule is therefore not exactly what was tested.
  • FIRMS detections include crop and stubble burning, which can happen on cooler days. Same-day comparison only: no lead time was tested.

Fire-day probability model (version 2): back-test only

Version 2 asks a different question: on a given day, how likely is it that NASA FIRMS records at least one detection (confidence 50 or higher) inside a northern governorate? It is a probability for a governorate-day. It says nothing about where or when a fire starts.

  • Data. One row per governorate per day, 1 June to 30 September, 2003 to 2025, for the same 11 northern governorates (30,866 rows). ERA5-Land reanalysis gives maximum temperature, humidity, rain, mean wind, and rain over the previous 7 and 30 days. The summer of 2018 has 2 missing FIRMS days out of 122. Script: scripts/08_fire_training_table.js. The table is in results/fire_training_table_2003_2025.csv.
  • Split. Trained on 2003 to 2020 (24,156 rows), tested on 2021 to 2025 (6,710 rows, the same days as the version 1 test). Nothing was tuned on the test years. The gradient boosting settings were chosen by cross-validation across the training years.

Version 1 is scored with fire rates taken from the test years themselves, which favours it slightly.

  • Version 2 ranks days clearly better than version 1. Per-year AUC for version 2 is 0.80, 0.73, 0.83, 0.85 and 0.84 for 2021 to 2025, so it did not collapse in any year. The weakest year is 2022.
  • Most of the skill comes from where (some governorates, such as Bizerte and Béja, have far more detections than others, partly because they are larger and more wooded) and when in summer. Weather adds a smaller part: AUC rises by 0.013 to 0.043 over governorate plus day of year (95% interval from resampling days).
  • Gradient boosting did not beat logistic regression (AUC difference between about −0.01 and +0.01), so the simpler model is kept.
  • Calibration is rough but usable. In the highest-rated tenth of days the model predicted 25% and 27% had a detection. In the middle bands it predicts too high (13% predicted against 10% observed in the ninth tenth). See results/fire_model_v2_calibration.csv.

Limits of version 2. It is not used in the app. It was trained and tested on reanalysis weather, not on forecasts, so its skill with real forecasts is unknown and will be lower. It covers northern governorates only. FIRMS detections include crop burning. A governorate counts as having a fire if there is a detection anywhere inside it, so size matters. Humidity and wind here are defined differently from the live app (daily mean wind here, daily maximum in the app). The 95% intervals ignore the link between neighbouring days and are too narrow.

6. Known limits

  • No ignition or flood-date prediction. Sparks and the exact rain intensity at a place cannot be forecast. Flash floods depend on local intensity, soil wetness and drainage that this app cannot see.
  • Forecasts lose skill with time. Days beyond about a week are weak for a specific place.
  • Fire-danger watch has weak skill. In the back-test its weather rule beat chance overall but missed 80% of days with a detection and was right on fewer than 1 flagged day in 10 (section 5). A better back-tested model (version 2) exists but is not in the app because it has not been tested with real forecasts.
  • River flow is coarse. The model uses a 5 km grid and the nearest river cell, and many Tunisian wadis flow only after storms, so values can be missing or wrong. Flow advice appears only above a demo minimum of 5 m³/s.
  • Resolution. One cell is about 1 km. Small fields, streets, wadis and small fires are not resolved.
  • Satellite gaps. Cloud, sea and areas outside Tunisia have no data. The 2025 monthly values are medians and can hide short events.
  • Clicked places are approximate. Names come from OpenStreetMap and may be wrong or missing. Regional watches use one point per governorate, so conditions elsewhere in the governorate can differ.
  • El Niño banner is typed in by hand from NOAA and shows a warning when it is out of date. Its effect on Tunisian rain is weak and uncertain.
  • Climate-vegetation zones describe 2025 only, agree only modestly with WorldCover (only slightly better than an NDVI-only split), and can absorb small features (see section 4).
